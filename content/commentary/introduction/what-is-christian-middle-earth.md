Since the Enlightenment, a yawning gap has grown between the study of the Bible in the pews of Christ's Church and in the ivory towers of academia. Due blame for this chasm could be assigned to both the intellectual slothfulness of many modern Christians and the pride of enlightened scholars, who often seek credibility within a system of study that relies on naturalistic presuppositions

Regardless of who is to blame for this gap, there exists a subset of individuals who find themselves squarely within it. These "betweeners" and the space they inhabit comprise what has come to be called *Christian Middle-earth* (CME). Dr. Michael Heiser, who first coined the term, describes it when he writes:

> Christian Middle Earth is that realm between actual biblical scholars (people with real credentials who write for peer review — and mostly write for themselves) and the largest realm, the local church, where serious biblical content is like a Bigfoot sighting.[^1]

By "in-between," I do not mean to suggest a state of lukewarm compromise, unwilling either to stand with dogmatic truth claims or to embrace the blasphemous assertions of New Atheism. Rather, these individuals find themselves between the target demographics of book publishers shilling the hopelessly shallow slop that fills the shelves of most Christian bookstores and the highly specialized world of academic works and Ph.D. dissertations, laden with niche terminology and technical jargon. They have grown dissatisfied with the brushing aside of challenging questions that arise from reading the Bible. Such questions are often discouraged or quickly dismissed with ready-made answers and theological safeguards. They wonder with dismay why large swaths of their Bibles are skimmed over, if addressed at all, from the pulpits of their churches.

Unable to find answers to their questions within the ecclesiastical setting, and lacking access to the scholarly literature that addresses such issues, Christian Middle-earthers often turn to less-than-ideal sources for answers. Pseudoscience, conspiracy theories, ghost hunters, Bible codes, and end-times predictions are all standard fare. Heiser estimates that "three-quarters of what gets taught there doesn't have a prayer of being correct."

It is because of this that CME has drawn criticism from Alastair Roberts, among others, for being an unhealthy breeding ground for *curiositas*.[^2] While much of Roberts's characterization of CME is undeniably true, it is with this subset of the church that we identify. Not because we relish wild speculation or the treatment of unsubstantiated ideas as authoritative, but because we believe that the questions asked there are worth wrestling with. In Scripture itself, we see not an avoidance of the strange nor a retreat behind pat theological answers, but rather a willingness to wrestle with the most challenging questions.

We know there are many Christians, and a growing number at that, who, like us, long to wrestle with the challenging questions of reality while being guided by the wisdom of God's eternal Word. We have found them among the pews of our local assemblies, spanning surprisingly eclectic demographics. We have seen them gather in Facebook groups and Discord servers. We have spent many late nights with them around campfires.

If you have read the Bible, you know that it contains weird, thorny, and, at times, for many Christians, even embarrassing passages. Those of us who claim this book as our sacred text should not shy away from these portions of Scripture. Rather, we should press into them and seek to understand them in humility. We should not seek to conform the Word to our tidy theological systems. Instead, as James instructs, we should seek God's wisdom as we ask these questions, doing so with faithful loyalty, not judging[^3] the text, but submitting ourselves to it.

While we use the term CME to refer specifically to believing Christians, we also recognize a growing movement within broader society that, in various ways, is beginning to acknowledge the poverty of the naturalistic worldview. It was once a relatively safe assumption that if someone in the West was not a Christian, it was because they believed that the observable universe was all that exists. That is no longer the case. What this means for the topic at hand is that, in today's marketplace of ideas, there is a growing number of competing answers on offer to the existential and fringe questions that many people are asking. Christians who pursue what we believe are worthy questions are encountering unworthy answers among the growing segments of society that are dabbling in ancient and evil practices and traditions.

It is for these reasons that we want to share with CME the riches we have found in the study of Genesis 1-11. This commentary is by no means a lone voice. We have benefited from, and are drawing upon, many voices, old and new, that have illuminated these same truths. Those works have been a blessing to us amid the fog of CME, and we hope that this work will likewise be a blessing to believers in the liminal space of Christian Middle-earth who desire to understand God's Word on its own terms.
